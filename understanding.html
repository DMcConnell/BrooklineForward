Understanding Brookline's
Financial Challenge

A plain-English briefing on what's driving the structural deficit, what the town has already done about it, and what the E&RSC's 2026 study found.

Last updated March 2026 Based on E&RSC Final Report, March 13, 2026

The Core Problem

Brookline's operating budget is structurally imbalanced — not because of poor management or waste, but because revenues and expenses are governed by different rules and grow at fundamentally different rates.

Property taxes make up approximately 79% of all operating revenue. Under Proposition 2½ — Massachusetts' 1980 tax limitation law — the property tax levy can only grow by 2.5% per year, plus a small increment for new development. In practice, this means Brookline's total levy grows at roughly 3% annually in real terms.

Expenses don't have a corresponding cap. The major cost drivers — employee healthcare benefits, union-negotiated wages, debt service from school construction, and special education costs — consistently grow at 5–8% per year. The gap between those two numbers compounds over time. A 2-point annual gap on a $200M+ budget doesn't stay small.

The math in plain terms

If revenue grows at 3% and your largest expenses grow at 6%, you don't just fall behind — you fall behind faster every year. The only legal tools available are: cut services, raise fees, grow the commercial tax base, or ask voters to approve a property tax override.

The Revenue-Expenditure Gap

Brookline operates under the Town-School Partnership (TSP), established in 1994, which governs how total revenue is divided between municipal operations and the Public Schools of Brookline (PSB). Both sides of that equation face the same structural pressure.

The town has held four operating overrides since 2008 — in 2008, 2015, 2019, and 2022. Each one has been larger than the last. The intervals between them have also shortened: seven years between 2008 and 2015, four years between 2015 and 2019, three years between 2019 and 2022. The E&RSC's projections suggest a fifth override discussion was inevitable in the current budget cycle.

What's Actually Driving Costs

On the Town Side

Employee benefits are the biggest pressure. Benefits represent 21.9% of the operating budget — rising to 22.8% in FY27 — and are consuming virtually all new property tax revenue each year. This includes mandatory pension contributions and health insurance, both of which are largely outside the town's direct control year-to-year.

Debt service has grown dramatically: from $10.6M in FY18 to $49.4M in FY27 — nearly a five-fold increase in less than a decade. Three decades of school construction bonds are coming due simultaneously, and this was a known, unavoidable consequence of those capital investments.

Fire overtime persistently exceeds budget by approximately $800,000 per year — a structural issue tied to staffing minimums and union contract terms.

On the Schools Side

Healthcare premiums are the most significant lever. Brookline currently pays 83% of employee healthcare premiums — among the highest of any comparable community. The E&RSC's independent auditor called this a "budget buster." The median among comparable communities is closer to 75–78%. Reducing the employer share toward that range would generate material annual savings, but requires negotiation through collective bargaining.

Salary step increases affect roughly 50% of PSB educators annually, layered on top of contractual cost-of-living adjustments. Brookline educator median salaries already exceed comparable districts in most categories.

Non-resident special education costs represent approximately $4M per year in unreimbursed expenses. When METCO and Materials Fee students require special education services, Brookline bears the full cost — roughly $28,000 per student per year — with zero reimbursement from the student's home district or from the state. With approximately 144 non-resident students receiving SpEd services, this is a significant and arguably inequitable subsidy.

A note on financial management

An external audit by CliftonLarsonAllen (May 2025) identified a $1.1M payroll formula error, delayed vendor payments, and deficient budget controls in the Schools. These are serious concerns — but they are distinct from the structural deficit. Fixing them matters, but it doesn't close a $22M gap.

What the E&RSC Found

The Expenditures & Revenues Study Committee published its 162-page final report on March 13, 2026, after a year of intensive work. It represents the most thorough independent review of Brookline's finances in recent memory.

The report's key findings confirm what the budget history already suggested: the structural deficit is real, it is compounding, and it cannot be resolved through management improvements alone. Both Town and Schools have already made meaningful cuts. This is a revenue-structure problem.

The Override Recommendation

The E&RSC recommended placing an override on the May 2026 ballot using a tiered structure — giving voters a choice between a full ask, a partial ask, and no override. This design is intended to maximize the probability that some level of override passes, rather than risking complete failure on a binary question.

The three tiers analyzed: Option 1 ($23.25M over three years, binary yes/no), Option 2 ($18.6M, front-loaded), and Option 3 (tiered ballot question with proportional cuts between Town and Schools at each tier — the committee's recommendation).

Nine Conditions for the Schools

The E&RSC's Schools Subcommittee attached nine specific structural reform conditions to its override support — requiring formal commitment via Memorandum of Understanding from the Town, School Committee, and PSB:

  1. Negotiate healthcare premium employer contribution down from 83% toward comparable-community levels by end of calendar 2028.
  2. Create a mechanism to manage wage expense growth toward year-over-year revenue growth.
  3. Commission a formal study of a middle school model and classroom consolidation, beginning in FY27.
  4. Complete implementation of MUNIS as the shared town-school financial system by end of FY27.
  5. Verify all teaching assignments comply with contractual terms.
  6. Audit and reduce technology leases and ed-tech subscriptions, especially in lower grades.
  7. Require all third-party users of school space between 7am–5:30pm to pay fair-market rent.
  8. Launch a facilities governance and youth athletics coordination pilot in FY27.
  9. Move the BEEP (early education) revolving fund toward full-cost break-even.

Longer-Term Opportunities the E&RSC Flagged

The committee's Future Subcommittee identified several longer-term opportunities it didn't have time to fully analyze:

  • Solar: A $3.2M capital investment across 5 rooftop sites could generate $390K/year in avoided electricity costs, with a full $25M program potentially covering 82% of town electricity needs.
  • Economic development: The committee recommended dedicating a Select Board member to economic development, elevating the planning division, and engaging major landowners proactively. The CHCA project alone was projected to generate approximately $5.6M/year in net new tax revenue at full build-out.
  • School consolidation: Five scenarios were modeled for school model changes that could free building space, increase class sizes, and reduce staffing costs over time.
  • The 2030 pension transition: The committee explicitly questioned whether redirecting the full ~$40M pension contribution to OPEB is optimal policy, but did not have time to model alternatives rigorously.

What This Means

Brookline faces a genuine fiscal challenge — one that reflects a structural mismatch between a 1980 tax limitation law and the realities of 21st-century municipal costs. It is not a failure of values or governance. The people who work for Brookline — in Town Hall, in the schools, in the fire department — are largely doing their jobs well.

What's needed isn't better management of the existing system. It's a deliberate effort to change the structural conditions: grow the commercial tax base, recover costs that are currently subsidized by Brookline for other municipalities, and make smart decisions about the major financial transition coming in 2030 when the pension obligation is fully funded.

That's what BrooklineForward is researching. The E&RSC did essential work identifying the problem and setting conditions. We're trying to build the long-term plan that goes alongside it.
