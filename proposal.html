The Three-Track
Research Framework

How we've organized the research — three connected tracks that together address the structural conditions behind Brookline's fiscal challenge.

Working framework — updated as research progresses

Brookline's fiscal challenge is structural. That means addressing it requires structural changes — not just better budgeting in the near term, but deliberate work on the underlying conditions that cause revenues and expenses to diverge year after year.

We've organized our research around three tracks that correspond to the three most significant structural levers available to Brookline. They're connected — action in each area reinforces the others. And none of them is a quick fix: this is research toward a 10-year framework, not a solution to this year's budget.

What this document is

This is a working framework, not a finished proposal. The research on each track is underway. Estimates and recommendations will be refined as data is gathered. See the research tracker for current task status.

Track 1 — Economic Development

Commercial property in Brookline is taxed at up to 175% of the residential rate — and unlike residential development, commercial growth doesn't generate proportional increases in school enrollment or demand for town services. It is one of the highest-leverage fiscal tools available to any municipality.

Brookline has historically underinvested in the governance and strategy required to realize this potential. The E&RSC's Future Subcommittee estimated that the Chestnut Hill Commercial Area (CHCA) project alone — when fully built out and stabilized, approximately 7+ years out — could generate roughly $5.6M per year in net new tax revenue. That's one project. A systematic strategy across all developable commercial sites could be transformational over a 10-year horizon.

What We're Researching

  • Commercial site inventory: Mapping every significant development opportunity — current assessed value, zoning constraints, ownership, and realistic timeline.
  • Fiscal impact modeling: Using the RKG Fiscal Impact Model (which the Planning Department has access to) to estimate net annual revenue per site.
  • Zoning reform: Identifying specific changes to FAR limits, height restrictions, and permitted uses that would unlock the highest-value stalled sites — with Cambridge and Somerville's as-of-right zoning reforms as reference models.
  • PILOT program: Designing a structured Payments-In-Lieu-Of-Taxes program for Brookline's large non-profit landholders, building on existing agreements like 2 Brookline Place and Newbury on Fisher Hill.
  • Asset monetization: Auditing municipal parking, the golf course, and school facilities for off-hours rental revenue that is currently being left on the table.
  • Out-of-district SpEd expansion: Modeling the revenue potential from expanding Brookline's specialized programs (Winthrop House, ALC, LAHB) to accept tuition-paying students from neighboring districts.
Estimated 10-year potential

$8–15M per year at full build-out, phased over 5–10 years. These are preliminary estimates pending full site analysis. See Track 1 research page →

Track 2 — State & Legislative Options

Several aspects of Brookline's fiscal picture are set by state law, not local policy. That means some of the most impactful potential changes require action at the State House rather than (or in addition to) action in Town Meeting.

The most concrete and quantifiable of these is the treatment of non-resident special education costs. Under current law, when METCO students or Materials Fee students require special education services, the receiving district — Brookline — bears the full cost with no reimbursement from the student's home district or from the state. The E&RSC identified approximately 144 non-resident students receiving SpEd services in Brookline at roughly $28,000 per student per year, representing approximately $4M annually in unreimbursed costs.

Newton, Wellesley, and Lexington face the same dynamic. Together, these communities would have a data-backed, multiparty case for legislative relief — either through an amendment to the Education Reform Act, a budget line item, or a Home Rule petition.

What We're Researching

  • Non-resident SpEd cost quantification: Building a rigorous, defensible estimate of Brookline's unreimbursed costs using DESE data — both to establish the magnitude of the issue and to construct a credible legislative argument.
  • Coalition mapping: Researching how Newton, Wellesley, and Lexington are affected, identifying CFO and superintendent contacts, and estimating the statewide aggregate cost across all METCO-receiving districts.
  • Legislative pathway: Identifying existing bills, potential champions in the state delegation, and the most viable legislative vehicle (budget line item, Ed Reform amendment, or Home Rule).
  • Chapter 70 analysis: Assessing whether Brookline is receiving its fair allocation under the state's education aid formula relative to its foundation budget obligations.
  • New revenue tools: Researching what local option revenue tools — real estate transfer tax, short-term rental surcharge, etc. — have been successfully authorized for other Massachusetts communities and what the path to Brookline adoption would look like.
Estimated potential

$4–8M per year in recovered non-resident SpEd costs, if legislation passes. Legislative timeline is inherently uncertain — this is a multi-year effort. See Track 2 research page →

Track 3 — The 2030 Financial Transition

In 2030, Brookline is on track to fully fund its pension liability — a full decade ahead of the state mandate. This is a genuine fiscal achievement, decades in the making. It also creates an immediate, high-stakes policy question: what happens to the approximately $40M per year that has been flowing into pension contributions?

The current Select Board policy — recently reaffirmed — is to redirect all of that $40M into funding OPEB (Other Post-Employment Benefits, primarily retiree healthcare). The intention is to eliminate the OPEB unfunded liability on the same compressed, proactive schedule used for pensions.

The E&RSC's own report explicitly questioned this approach. OPEB costs are growing faster than assumed, there is no state mandate to fully fund OPEB by any date, and Brookline will still have a structural operating deficit in 2030. Simply redirecting the full $40M to OPEB — while continuing to rely on operating overrides to fund services — may not be the optimal policy.

No one has yet built a rigorous model of the alternatives. That's what this track does.

The Four Scenarios We're Modeling

  • Scenario A: Redirect the full $40M to OPEB immediately starting FY31. Baseline/status quo policy.
  • Scenario B: Split the $40M — approximately $25M to OPEB, $15M to structural operating relief.
  • Scenario C: Dynamic allocation — tie the OPEB contribution to a formula that accounts for the deficit trajectory and economic development revenue as it comes online.
  • Scenario D: Healthcare reform first — achieve meaningful OPEB liability reduction through changes to the employer healthcare premium contribution (83% → GIC or comparable), then design the OPEB pivot from a stronger baseline.

What We're Researching

  • The current OPEB unfunded liability and the actuarially determined annual contribution to maintain (not worsen) it — from the GASB 74/75 report in the E&RSC appendix.
  • What level of OPEB funding Moody's would view as consistent with maintaining Brookline's Aaa credit rating — and whether Brookline has ever formally asked this question.
  • Estimated savings from joining the MA Group Insurance Commission (GIC) for employee health insurance at current enrollment levels.
  • How Newton, Wellesley, and Lexington have planned for their own pension-to-OPEB transitions.
Estimated potential (FY31+)

$10–20M per year in operating relief, depending on the scenario chosen and healthcare reform progress. This is the highest-magnitude lever — and the one with the most complex trade-offs. See Track 3 research page →

How the Three Tracks Interact

These aren't independent workstreams — they reinforce each other in specific ways that matter for both the analysis and the timing of any recommendations:

  • Track 1 → Track 3: Economic development revenue reduces override dependence over time, which makes a less aggressive OPEB scenario more financially viable without Moody's concern — because the structural deficit is shrinking from the revenue side.
  • Track 2 → Track 3: Recovering $4–8M in current-year non-resident SpEd costs directly reduces the deficit the 2030 OPEB pivot has to address — making all scenarios slightly more favorable.
  • Track 3 → Track 1: A well-designed OPEB pivot that frees operating funds starting FY31 could partially seed economic development investments — a land acquisition fund, infrastructure improvements, or developer incentives — that accelerate Track 1 revenue.

A Note on Timelines

None of these tracks produces results immediately. Economic development at scale takes 5–10 years. Legislative action takes 2–4 years at minimum. The OPEB pivot decision comes in 2030. Understanding the sequencing and interactions between these tracks — and what Brookline needs to do now to set each one in motion — is a core goal of the research.

This means BrooklineForward is not primarily about the current override cycle. It's about ensuring that whatever decision is made in 2026 is accompanied by a credible, data-grounded long-term plan — so that the next budget conversation can happen from a position of strategy rather than crisis.
