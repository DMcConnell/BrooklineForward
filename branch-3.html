Track 3: The 2030 Financial Transition

In 2030, Brookline fully funds its pension obligation — a genuine fiscal achievement. What happens next to the ~$40M per year currently flowing into pension contributions is one of the most consequential policy questions the town will face.

Why This Track Matters

Brookline has been making mandatory pension contributions for decades — and by 2030, that work is done. The pension liability will be fully funded, a full decade ahead of the state mandate. This is a genuine achievement that reflects years of disciplined fiscal management.

It also creates an immediate, high-stakes policy question: what happens to approximately $40M per year that will no longer need to flow into pension contributions?

The current Select Board policy — recently reaffirmed — is to redirect all of that $40M into funding OPEB (Other Post-Employment Benefits, primarily retiree healthcare). The intention is to eliminate the OPEB unfunded liability on the same compressed, proactive schedule used for pensions.

But the E&RSC's own report explicitly questioned this approach. OPEB costs are growing faster than assumed. There is no state mandate to fully fund OPEB by any date. And Brookline will still have a structural operating deficit in 2030. Redirecting the full $40M to OPEB — while continuing to rely on operating overrides to fund services — may not be the optimal policy.

No one has yet built a rigorous model of the alternatives. That's what this track does.

The magnitude of this decision

$40M per year is larger than a single operating override. How Brookline allocates this money starting in FY31 will shape the town's finances for a generation. Getting this decision right — with data, modeling, and community input — is worth serious effort now, while there's still time to influence it.

The Four Scenarios

We're modeling four distinct approaches to the 2030 transition. Each involves different trade-offs between OPEB funding discipline, structural operating relief, and fiscal risk.

Scenario A

Full OPEB redirect. Redirect the entire $40M to OPEB immediately starting FY31. This is the current Select Board policy — the baseline against which other scenarios are compared. It maximizes OPEB funding speed but provides zero near-term operating relief, meaning override dependence continues.

Scenario B

Split allocation. Approximately $25M to OPEB, $15M to structural operating relief. This directly reduces the size of future overrides, while still making meaningful progress on the OPEB liability. The key question is whether $25M/year satisfies Moody's for Aaa maintenance.

Scenario C

Dynamic allocation. Tie the OPEB contribution to a formula that accounts for the structural deficit trajectory and economic development revenue as it comes online. As Track 1 revenue materializes, less operating relief is needed from the pension pivot — freeing more for OPEB. More complex, but potentially optimal over a 10-year horizon.

Scenario D

Healthcare reform first. Achieve meaningful OPEB liability reduction through changes to the employer healthcare premium contribution (currently 83% → GIC or comparable rate), then design the OPEB pivot from a stronger baseline. Healthcare savings reduce the OPEB liability growth rate, potentially making a less aggressive contribution schedule viable.

Key Questions This Research Will Answer

Question 1

What is Brookline's current OPEB unfunded liability, and what is the actuarially determined annual contribution required to maintain (not worsen) it — as reported in the most recent GASB 74/75 actuarial valuation?

Question 2

What level of OPEB funding would Moody's view as consistent with maintaining Brookline's Aaa credit rating — and has Brookline ever formally asked this question? This is the binding constraint on any scenario other than A.

Question 3

What are the estimated annual savings from joining the MA Group Insurance Commission (GIC) for employee health insurance, at current Brookline enrollment levels?

Question 4

How have Newton, Wellesley, and Lexington planned for their own pension-to-OPEB transitions — and what constraints have they faced?

Question 5

Under Scenario B or C, what is the fiscal gap in FY31 — accounting for structural deficit trajectory, estimated Track 1 revenue, and Track 2 legislative proceeds — and what override or levy pressure remains?

Research Workstreams

OPEB Liability Baseline

Before modeling scenarios, we need a precise baseline: the current unfunded OPEB liability, the annual "actuarially determined contribution" (ADC), and how rapidly the liability is growing relative to current contributions. This comes from the GASB 74/75 actuarial report included in the E&RSC appendix. Getting this number right is foundational — all four scenarios are calibrated against it.

Scenario Financial Modeling

Building a 10-year model (FY31–FY41) for each of the four scenarios, tracking: OPEB funded ratio, structural operating deficit trajectory, cumulative override requirement, and total tax levy impact for a median residential taxpayer. The goal is a clear side-by-side comparison that makes the trade-offs legible — not just to analysts, but to Town Meeting members and the public.

Moody's Sensitivity Analysis

Brookline holds a Aaa credit rating from Moody's — one of fewer than 40 municipalities in the country to do so. This rating has real fiscal value (lower borrowing costs). Any scenario that reduces OPEB contributions below Scenario A risks scrutiny from Moody's. We're researching what OPEB funding levels are associated with Aaa maintenance in comparable communities, and whether there is documented guidance from Moody's on this question.

Healthcare Reform Savings Model

Brookline currently pays 83% of employee healthcare premiums — among the highest of any comparable community. The median is closer to 75–78%. Moving toward GIC rates or comparable levels would reduce both current operating costs and the forward trajectory of the OPEB liability (since OPEB is fundamentally a healthcare benefits obligation). This workstream models the savings under several scenarios and their compounding effect on the OPEB liability.

Comparable Community Strategies

Newton, Wellesley, and Lexington all face the same pension transition question — some on slightly different timelines. We're researching how each community has approached their own transition planning, what policy commitments they've made publicly, and whether any have developed models Brookline could learn from or adapt.

How Track 3 Connects to the Other Tracks

The 2030 transition doesn't happen in isolation. Its optimal design depends on what Track 1 and Track 2 deliver:

If Track 1 (economic development) begins generating significant new tax revenue by FY33–35, the structural deficit is smaller — which means less operating relief is needed from the pension pivot, which means more can go to OPEB. Scenario C (dynamic allocation) is designed precisely to capture this interaction.

If Track 2 (legislative) successfully recovers $4–8M in non-resident SpEd costs, that directly reduces the operating deficit the FY31 pivot has to address — making Scenarios B, C, and D more viable without threatening the Aaa rating.

And a well-designed pivot that frees $10–15M in operating funds starting FY31 could itself seed economic development investments — a land acquisition fund, infrastructure improvements, or developer incentives — that accelerate Track 1 revenue. The interactions run in both directions.
