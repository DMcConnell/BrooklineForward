Track 1: Economic Development

Researching how Brookline can grow its commercial tax base — through new development, smarter use of existing assets, and structured partnerships with non-profit landholders.

Why This Track Matters

Commercial property in Brookline is taxed at a higher rate than residential property — and unlike new homes, commercial development doesn't generate proportional increases in school enrollment or demand for town services like fire, parks, and libraries. That asymmetry makes commercial growth one of the highest-leverage fiscal tools available to Brookline over the long term.

The opportunity is significant. The Chestnut Hill Commercial Area (CHCA) redevelopment project — just one site — was estimated by the E&RSC's Future Subcommittee to generate approximately $5.6M per year in net new tax revenue when fully built out and stabilized (a roughly 7-year timeline from groundbreaking). That's one project. A systematic approach across the town's developable commercial corridors could be substantially larger.

At the same time, Brookline holds significant non-property assets — parking facilities, a golf course, school buildings with substantial unused capacity — that currently generate less revenue than comparable municipal assets elsewhere. And Brookline's large non-profit landholders — hospitals, educational institutions, religious organizations — collectively hold significant tax-exempt property on which the town receives nothing.

Key Questions This Research Will Answer

Question 1

What is Brookline's current commercial assessed value as a percentage of total assessed value, and how does this compare to Newton, Wellesley, and Lexington?

Question 2

Which specific parcels represent the highest-value near-term development opportunities, and what zoning changes does each require?

Question 3

What is the realistic capacity of PSB's specialized SpEd programs to absorb out-of-district students without compromising service quality?

Question 4

What is the full inventory of non-profit-held real property in Brookline, and what would fair PILOTs look like for the top 20 holders?

Question 5

What have Cambridge and Somerville done with as-of-right zoning that Brookline could replicate, and what was the revenue impact?

Question 6

What would a dedicated economic development governance structure actually look like — and what would it cost to run?

Research Workstreams

Commercial Site Inventory

The foundation of this track is a complete inventory of Brookline's commercial development opportunities — pulled from Assessor's Office data and analyzed using the RKG Fiscal Impact Model that the Planning Department already has access to. The goal is a prioritized pipeline showing which sites have the highest fiscal impact potential, what their current status is, and what the realistic timeline to revenue looks like.

Zoning Reform

Several high-value Brookline sites are effectively undevelopable under current zoning — FAR limits, height restrictions, and use categories that made sense decades ago but now constrain the town's economic potential. We're mapping those constraints against the sites in the inventory and researching what targeted zoning changes have worked in comparable communities.

PILOT Program

Brookline already has a handful of PILOT (Payment In Lieu Of Taxes) agreements with non-profit institutions. We're researching the full landscape — what the town currently receives, what comparable communities have negotiated, and what a structured, incentive-based PILOT framework for Brookline's top 20 non-profit landholders could realistically generate.

Asset Monetization

Municipal parking, the golf course, and school buildings represent assets the town owns and operates — but may not be optimizing from a revenue standpoint. We're benchmarking Brookline's current revenue from each against comparable municipal assets to identify gaps.

Out-of-District SpEd Expansion

Brookline runs several highly regarded specialized special education programs — Winthrop House, the ALC, LAHB — that neighboring districts cannot match and currently pay Brookline tuition rates to access. We're modeling whether deliberately expanding capacity in these programs to serve more out-of-district students could generate meaningful net revenue, and what the operational requirements would be.
