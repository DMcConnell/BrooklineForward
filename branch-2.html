Why This Track Matters

Not every lever in Brookline's fiscal situation is controlled locally. State law shapes how education aid is calculated, how special education costs are assigned, and what revenue tools municipalities are permitted to use. That means some of the most impactful changes require action at the State House — and require Brookline to engage as an advocate, not just a recipient.

The most concrete near-term opportunity is the treatment of non-resident special education costs. Brookline participates in the METCO program, accepting students from Boston and other urban communities — a mission the town is proud of. But when those students, or Materials Fee non-residents, require special education services, current law assigns 100% of the cost to Brookline with no reimbursement from the student's home district or the state.

The E&RSC identified approximately 144 non-resident students currently receiving SpEd services in Brookline at roughly $28,000 per student per year — approximately $4M annually. Newton, Wellesley, and Lexington face the identical situation. Together, these communities represent a compelling, data-backed case for legislative relief.

Important distinction

This track is not about reducing Brookline's commitment to METCO or to students with special needs. It's about whether the state — which designed this system — should reimburse the costs it creates for receiving districts. These are separate questions.

Key Questions This Research Will Answer

Question 1

How many METCO-receiving communities exist in Massachusetts, and what is the aggregate unreimbursed SpEd cost statewide? This is the foundation of any legislative argument.

Question 2

What bills are currently alive at the State House addressing Prop 2½, Chapter 70, or METCO reimbursement — and who are the champions?

Question 3

Is Brookline receiving its fair allocation under the Chapter 70 foundation budget formula — or is it effectively subsidizing the state's underfunding?

Question 4

What is the Home Rule petition process, and has Brookline used it successfully for revenue-enabling legislation in the past?

Question 5

What is Brookline's relationship with its State House delegation, and how has that delegation historically prioritized municipal fiscal issues?

Research Workstreams

Non-Resident Special Education Costs

The first task is building a rigorous, defensible estimate of the unreimbursed cost — using DESE's public per-pupil cost data, Brookline's enrollment records, and a clearly stated methodology. Getting this number right matters: it's the core financial claim in any legislative argument, and it will be scrutinized.

Coalition Mapping

Researching Newton, Wellesley, and Lexington's METCO enrollment and SpEd costs to quantify the shared burden — and to identify the right contacts for a coordinated approach. Also estimating the statewide aggregate to frame the issue at scale.

Legislative Strategy

Mapping the current State House landscape: Brookline's delegation (Representative + Senator), any existing bills on METCO reimbursement or related topics, and what legislative vehicle (budget line item, Education Reform amendment, or Home Rule petition) gives the best combination of speed and durability.

Chapter 70 Reform

Massachusetts' Chapter 70 education aid formula determines how much state funding Brookline receives for its schools. We're analyzing whether Brookline's foundation budget calculation accurately reflects its actual costs — particularly healthcare costs, which the formula has historically underweighted.

New Revenue Tools

Several Massachusetts communities have pursued or obtained authority for local option revenue tools — most notably a real estate transfer tax, which Boston and Cambridge have long advocated for, and short-term rental surcharges. We're researching the current legislative status of each and what a Brookline path would look like.
